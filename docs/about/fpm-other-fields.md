# Methods from other fields

The methods FPM is built from are in the [design notes](https://github.com/filippospol/FPM/blob/main/docs/about/fpm-design.md), and the directions that extend the model are in [future work](https://github.com/filippospol/FPM/blob/main/docs/about/fpm-future-work.md). This file is the narrower case: methods from other fields, physics, probability and operations research among them, with no public basketball application we could find. They are candidate ideas rather than established tools, which is the whole point of keeping them apart. None is in the model. Each entry says what the method is and how the data already in this repository connects to it, and where one has been tried on that data, what the test showed and no more.

The data is the same as everywhere else here: twenty seasons of play-by-play, stints with possessions and points for both sides, every shot with coordinates and a zone, box scores, and player and coach fields. Where a method needs player tracking the repository does not have, the entry says so.
## Maximum-entropy models of lineup synergy

When you know some averages of a system but not its full joint distribution, the maximum-entropy principle says to take the distribution that matches those averages and assumes nothing else, the flattest one consistent with what you measured. Jaynes set this out as the honest default: any sharper distribution would be smuggling in structure the data did not give you. The useful case is when the constraints are each unit's mean and the pairwise correlations, because then the maximum-entropy distribution is exactly an Ising model, a field term for each unit and a coupling term for each pair. Schneidman and colleagues ran this on populations of retinal neurons and found that weak pairwise correlations, fed into a pairwise maximum-entropy model, reproduced the firing patterns of the whole population, with couplings stronger than the small correlations would suggest. The couplings are the interaction the parts do not show on their own.

A lineup is five units on the floor at once, which is the same shape as a handful of neurons firing together. FPM reads a lineup as the sum of five individual ratings, and the design notes record where that reading breaks, over guard-short fives, and file it as a missing synergy term. A pairwise maximum-entropy model fit on the stints turns that term into estimated couplings between players: hold each player's solo effect as the field, let the data set the pair interactions as the couplings, and whatever additivity cannot account for lands in the couplings rather than in the residual. It is the principled way to ask how much of a lineup's result is interaction instead of sum, and which pairs carry it, from the possession data already here. The same object the synergy term in future work is after, reached from the physics side. Reading: Jaynes, *Information theory and statistical mechanics*, Physical Review (1957); Schneidman, Berry, Segev & Bialek, [*Weak pairwise correlations imply strongly correlated network states in a neural population*](https://arxiv.org/abs/q-bio/0512013) (2006).

Tried on the stint data, with each lineup's five published FPMs as the fields and the four position tiers as the units, the fitted couplings come out sub-additive for frontcourt pairs, most sharply for two bigs, which is the same shape as the guard-short shortfall the design notes record, and close to its measured size. Held out season by season, the correction helps on the rare frontcourt-heavy lineups it is built for and barely moves the rest, which is what the notes expect of it. It changes how five ratings are summed into a lineup, not the ratings themselves, and it leans on a thin sample of unusual fives, with the one-guard case not matching cleanly. So it reads as promising for the lineup arithmetic the notes flag, with whether it would help the published model left open rather than claimed.

## Random matrix theory and the Marchenko-Pastur bound

Estimate a correlation matrix from N series of length T and, when T is not far larger than N, most of what you are looking at is noise. Random matrix theory says how much. The Marchenko-Pastur law gives the eigenvalue spectrum you would see from pure noise, strictly uncorrelated series, as a function of the ratio N over T, and it has a sharp upper edge. Eigenvalues that fall inside that edge are consistent with noise and carry no reliable information; the few that poke out above it are the real structure. Laloux and colleagues brought this into finance, showing that the bulk of an empirical stock correlation matrix sits inside the Marchenko-Pastur band and should not be trusted, and that cleaning the matrix down to its handful of genuine eigenvalues improves everything built on top of it.

The design notes admit the thin-sample problem in plain terms: the season is short, rotations are tight, the same five recur, and the regression cannot cleanly separate players who almost always share the floor. That is an N-near-T problem, the regime Marchenko-Pastur was written for. Applied to the player-by-player covariance drawn from the stints, or to the correlation structure of the RAPM design matrix, the bound separates the lineup co-variation that is real from the part that is small-sample noise, and a cleaned matrix is a more honest input to the ridge. It gives a way to measure how much of the collinearity the notes worry about is signal, and to strip the rest, from the data already in hand. Reading: Marchenko & Pastur, *Distribution of eigenvalues for some sets of random matrices*, Matematicheskii Sbornik (1967); Laloux, Cizeau, Bouchaud & Potters, [*Noise dressing of the financial correlation matrices*](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/9810255) (1999).

Computed across the seasons, the spectrum behaves as the finance work predicts: only a few dozen directions sit above the noise band and the rest form a long collinear tail, which makes it a clean way to see how few real degrees of freedom a EuroLeague season holds. Used the other way, to clean that covariance and refit, it did not beat the plain ridge the model already leans on, and came out behind it on every season pair tried out of sample. The reason is structural: the finance recipe keeps the large eigenvalues and flattens the small ones, while the player separation a rating needs lives in the small directions, which is exactly where the ridge and the box prior already do the work. So it reads here as a useful diagnostic rather than a lift to the estimate.

## Retrial queue of offensive rebounds

A retrial queue, from the theory of service and teletraffic systems, is one in which a customer who cannot be served does not leave but joins an orbit and tries again. What governs it is the chance of re-entry on each failed attempt, and when that chance is constant the orbit is memoryless and the number of tries is geometric. The branching ratio, the expected number of retries each attempt sets off, says whether the orbit stays small or runs away.

A possession is a service attempt and a missed shot is its failure, and the offensive rebound is the retrial: the ball stays with the offense and the possession re-enters the orbit for another try, while a defensive rebound or a turnover is the customer leaving. Everything it needs is in the play-by-play, the shot attempts and the offensive rebounds inside each possession, so the re-entry probability and the branching ratio are read straight off the data. Reading: Falin & Templeton, *Retrial Queues* (1997); Artalejo & Gomez-Corral, *Retrial Queueing Systems: A Computational Approach* (2008).

On the data here the offensive-rebound loop fits the retrial model almost exactly. A miss is offensively rebounded about a third of the time, the branching ratio sits near 0.17 and barely moves across nineteen seasons, and the defining property holds every year: a possession is as likely to extend by another shot whether it is the first, second or third attempt, which is the memoryless signature, and the count of shots per possession lands on the geometric prediction to within a fraction of a percent. The clean fit is the result, and it carries one plain basketball reading, that second chances neither compound nor tire, each miss an independent draw at another shot. It does not improve the ratings, because offensive rebounding is already inside them twice, once in the possession-level score the regression is fit on and once as a box feature in the prior. It stands as a clean description of how a possession is built, and the branching ratio as an era-stable number, rather than a lift to the model.
